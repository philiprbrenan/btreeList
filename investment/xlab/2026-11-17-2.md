# Database on a Chip

**X-Lab Team:** Database on a Chip

**Technical Lead:** Philip R. Brenan
**Written Proposal to the NSF X-Labs Initiative**
**Topic: Computation at the Limit of Physics**

# 1. Mission

### Vision

To develop and validate a new computing architecture in which data structures and the operations performed on them are implemented together in specialized silicon, reducing the overhead of moving data between separate processing and memory components.

Modern computers can perform arithmetic operations extremely quickly, yet moving data between processors and memory remains costly in latency, bandwidth and energy. This is particularly important in database processing, searching, indexing, financial transactions and large-scale information retrieval, where algorithms repeatedly access structured data and may spend substantial resources on memory access, address calculation and control flow rather than on the underlying computation.

Database on a Chip proposes a different approach.

Rather than treating the data structure as software maintained by a general-purpose processor, Database on a Chip makes the structure and the logic that operates on it the primary units of hardware design. The objective is to keep data and its associated operations physically close, reducing avoidable transfers and general-purpose instruction overhead.

The intended result is a class of programmable, application-specialized computing devices in which operations are performed within, or closely coupled to, the hardware representation of the data structure.

Our initial platform will implement B-tree database structures, including the operations required to search, insert, delete and maintain them. B-trees are particularly suitable as an initial demonstration because they are widely used, have well-defined algorithms, involve substantial data movement in conventional implementations, and support a broad range of practical applications.

However, the mission extends well beyond B-trees.

We intend to establish a reusable architecture and development platform for translating well-defined data structures and their associated algorithms into efficient silicon implementations. The research will identify which structures benefit from this approach, which do not, and what architectural principles can be reused across applications.

The long-term objective is to make it practical to construct computers in which substantial portions of what are currently software-intensive data-processing systems are implemented directly in hardware.

### Why an X-Lab is necessary

The development of Database on a Chip requires coordinated advances in algorithms, computer architecture, digital logic, semiconductor implementation, memory technology and software tooling.

These disciplines are normally pursued in separate research and commercial environments.

Traditional computer architecture research tends to optimize processors and memory systems independently. Database research generally assumes conventional processors and memory hierarchies. Semiconductor development focuses on implementing established computational architectures efficiently.

Consequently, there is no natural institutional home for an effort whose primary objective is to eliminate the architectural separation between data structures, memory and processing.

An independently operated NSF X-Lab would bring these disciplines together around a common experimental programme: investigate the architecture, improve the design tools, produce and test hardware prototypes, and establish independently reproducible measurements of performance, area and energy. The X-Lab structure is valuable because it can sustain a focused effort across disciplines that are often funded and organized separately.

# 2. Technology Landscape

### The fundamental inefficiency

Conventional computing architectures separate processing and storage.

A processor retrieves data from memory, performs an operation and writes the result back. Complex data structures require many such operations, frequently involving indirect addressing, comparisons, branching and repeated memory accesses.

For database operations, this creates a substantial overhead.

For example, searching a B-tree involves traversing a sequence of branches, examining keys at each level, selecting the next node and eventually locating the requested record.

In a conventional computer, these operations involve repeated interaction between the processor and memory hierarchy.

The total cost of an operation therefore includes more than the comparisons themselves: it can include instruction fetch and decode, address generation, memory hierarchy accesses, data transfers and control overhead. The relative contribution of each component depends on the implementation and workload and must be measured rather than assumed.

Database on a Chip attacks this inefficiency at its architectural source.

Instead of moving the data to the processor, we bring the processing to the data.

### Existing approaches

Several established technologies address aspects of this problem:

- **Conventional processors:** Highly flexible and programmable, but dependent on separate memory hierarchies and associated data movement.
- **Application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs):** Offer substantial opportunities for specialization, but generally require significant investment in hardware design and implementation.
- **Field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs):** Permit customized processing architectures but introduce their own area, power and implementation constraints.
- **Processing-in-memory technologies:** Move selected computational operations closer to memory, but often retain restrictions on the supported operations and programming models.
- **Graphics processing units:** Provide considerable parallel processing capability, but are not necessarily efficient for irregular, pointer-based data structures and sequential search operations.

These approaches demonstrate that specialization and proximity to memory can improve computational efficiency.

Database on a Chip explores a complementary approach: make the data structure and its operations the starting point for hardware design, rather than adapting every operation to the instruction set and memory hierarchy of a general-purpose processor. Its scope and advantages must be established experimentally against optimized alternatives, including ASIC, FPGA and processing-in-memory implementations where appropriate.

### The proposed platform

Database on a Chip develops a design methodology that starts with conventional, understandable algorithms and progressively transforms them into silicon.

The existing development environment uses Java as the algorithmic specification language.

Algorithms are implemented and tested in Java, translated into Verilog, and then passed through established synthesis and physical-design tools.

This separates the algorithmic specification from its physical implementation and provides a common reference against which hardware behavior can be checked. The software and hardware versions can be exercised with corresponding inputs so that functional discrepancies can be detected during development.

The development platform incorporates:

1. A hardware-oriented representation of algorithms and data structures.
2. Automatic generation of Verilog from the algorithmic representation.
3. Translation of data storage requirements into appropriate hardware memory structures.
4. Integration with SRAM and ROM implementation tools.
5. Synthesis, placement and physical-design flows.
6. Automated comparison of software and hardware execution to identify functional discrepancies.

The architecture is designed to support reusable hardware components rather than requiring every new application to be designed from the transistor level.

### Preliminary comparison with a RISC-V implementation

A preliminary comparison has been made between the specialized Database on a Chip implementation and a generic RISC-V processor implementation using the same semiconductor technology node. Both were evaluated on the same B-tree search task, with two or three keys per leaf or branch.

The preliminary results reported by the project are:

|---|---:|---:|
| Silicon area | 2,467 ÂµmÂ² | 12,927 ÂµmÂ² |
| Maximum frequency | 902 MHz | 399 MHz |

On the reported measures, the specialized implementation has:

- 5.24 times less silicon area;
- 2.26 times higher maximum operating frequency;
- 5.30 times fewer executed statements.

Multiplying the three ratios gives a composite factor of approximately 62.76. This figure summarizes the differences in area, maximum frequency and executed-statement count; it is not a standard single performance metric, nor does it establish 62.76 times greater throughput or 62.76 times lower energy consumption. Area is not a direct measurement of power, and statement count is not by itself a measure of execution time. Throughput, latency, power and energy per completed operation must be measured separately under controlled, equivalent conditions.

Using the same technology node removes one important source of difference and makes the comparison useful evidence for the potential of architectural specialization. A rigorous follow-on comparison must also document and, as far as practicable, align the process libraries, design rules, operating conditions, timing constraints, memory assumptions, tool settings and workload. The present results are preliminary evidence, not yet a complete independent benchmark.

The Verilog implementation has been synthesized and physically routed using OpenROAD, and the B-tree search operation has also been demonstrated on an FPGA. These are meaningful implementation results, but they should not be confused with measurements from a fabricated ASIC. Silicon fabrication, laboratory characterization and direct power and energy measurements remain future work.

# 3. Outcomes

The X-Lab will test three connected propositions: whether the preliminary results can be reproduced under a fully documented and independently reviewable methodology; whether the architecture retains or increases its advantage as workloads and data structures become more demanding; and whether the approach can be generalized beyond B-trees.

The programme will be organized into stages with explicit deliverables and decision points. Dates are planning estimates and should be adjusted to the eventual award period.

### Stage 1: Establish a reproducible hardware baseline

**Indicative period: Years 1â2**

The initial objective is to establish a complete, independently reproducible implementation and benchmark environment. The work will use the existing open-source toolchain and, where access can be arranged, evaluate commercial EDA tools such as Cadence and Synopsys. Access to commercial tools is a capability to be secured through licensing, institutional collaboration or an industry partner; it is not assumed to be currently available to the project.

Deliverables:

- A complete B-tree implementation supporting search, insertion and deletion.
- Automated translation from Java to Verilog.
- Verified equivalence between software and hardware execution.
- Dedicated memory implementations using appropriate SRAM and ROM structures.
- A reproducible ASIC synthesis and physical-design flow.
- A documented reference design and benchmark suite.

The reference implementation will be compared with conventional processor-based implementations using identical datasets and equivalent operations.

Measurements will include:

- Silicon area.
- Clock frequency.
- Throughput.
- Energy per operation.
- Memory bandwidth.
- Data movement.
- Latency.
- Cost per completed operation.

A critical outcome will be a reliable measurement framework that distinguishes improvements in raw processing speed from improvements in actual energy efficiency.

### Stage 2: Optimize and quantify architectural advantages

**Indicative period: Years 2â3**

The second stage will concentrate on optimizing the architecture and establishing its practical advantages.

Research will investigate:

- Alternative B-tree layouts.
- Branching factors and node sizes.
- Parallel execution of independent operations.
- Memory organization and access patterns.
- Dedicated control logic.
- Reduction of unnecessary memory transfers.
- Integration of processing logic with storage.

The objective is to determine whether architectural optimization can deliver a substantial, reproducible reduction in energy per completed operation. An initial research target is an improvement of at least 10 times on selected workloads relative to appropriately optimized conventional implementations. This is a target to be tested, not a result already demonstrated. The benchmark definitions, comparison baseline and final quantitative target will be fixed after the Stage 1 measurements.

### Stage 3: Generalize the architecture

**Indicative period: Years 3â5**

The third stage will extend the platform beyond B-trees.

The objective is to demonstrate that the methodology can be applied to multiple independent classes of data structures and algorithms.

Candidate developments include:

- Hash tables.
- Other balanced search trees.
- Graph-processing structures.
- Priority queues.
- Associative lookup structures.
- Specialized indexing and retrieval engines.

The outcome will be a reusable hardware development environment rather than a collection of unrelated ASIC implementations.

We will also investigate more sophisticated memory architectures and opportunities for greater parallelism.

### Stage 4: Explore longer-term limits of efficient computation

**Indicative period: Years 5â7**

The final stage will investigate the potential for substantially greater improvements through architectural specialization, memory integration and alternative hardware implementation techniques.

The ambition is to contribute to the NSF objective of narrowing the gap between conventional CMOS computing and the inherent physical limits of computation.

For carefully defined classes of workloads, we will investigate the longer-term possibility of improvements of several orders of magnitude in useful work per unit of energy. Such gains are a research ambition, not a forecast or a capability demonstrated by the current design. Any claim of this scale would require a clearly defined workload, a credible optimized baseline and independently reproducible measurements.

This is an ambitious research target, not an asserted capability of the current implementation.

Achieving it will require more than incremental improvements in clock speed or transistor density. It will require identifying and eliminating unnecessary computational activity and data movement at every level of the architecture.

### Experimental method and decision points

Comparisons will use equivalent completed operations and documented workloads. Where possible, the reference processor will be optimized for the same task rather than treated as an intentionally weak baseline. Results will report area, clock frequency, latency, throughput, power and energy per operation separately. The project will also record memory configuration, data size, workload distribution, process and library assumptions, synthesis and physical-design constraints, and tool versions.

At the end of each stage, the X-Lab will review the evidence against pre-defined criteria and decide whether to scale the current design, change the architecture or redirect effort. This makes the programme an experimental research plan rather than a commitment to predetermined performance claims.

### Principal success criteria

The X-Lab will use the following measures to evaluate progress:

| Measure | Initial objective | Long-term ambition |
|---|---|---|
| Functional correctness | Exact agreement with reference algorithms | Generalized verified translation |
| Supported structures | B-tree | Multiple data structures |
| Energy efficiency | Establish measured baseline; target â¥10Ã on selected workloads | Investigate whether much larger gains are possible for narrowly defined workloads |
| Hardware implementation | Reproducible ASIC design | Multiple optimized silicon implementations |

| Applications | Database search and modification | Multiple data-intensive sectors |

All energy-efficiency claims will be supported by measured results under documented and comparable operating conditions.

The project will publish benchmark definitions, implementation details and measurement methodology wherever security and intellectual property considerations permit.

# 5. Team Capabilities Statement

### Complementary capabilities

Database on a Chip combines an existing working software and hardware development platform with a clearly defined architectural objective.

The project is unusual in that its central technical foundation has already been developed by its originator.

This provides a starting point for an X-Lab that can concentrate its resources on advancing and validating the technology rather than spending its initial years defining a research concept.

The X-Lab will assemble the following capabilities. At proposal stage, these should be understood as the capabilities required by the programme, not as a claim that every role is already filled:

1. **Architectural leadership:** Definition of the overall data-centric computing architecture and its evolution.
2. **Algorithm and software engineering:** Development and verification of data structures and their translation into hardware.
3. **Semiconductor engineering:** Optimization of logic, memory structures and physical implementations.
4. **Experimental validation:** Independent measurement of performance, energy efficiency and scalability.
5. **Commercial development:** Identification of applications, licensing opportunities and routes to adoption.

The objective is to create a small, technically focused organization with the ability to make rapid decisions and redirect research effort in response to experimental results.

### Governance and autonomy

During Phase 0, the project will operate under an appropriate lead-organization arrangement while establishing its technical leadership, recruitment plan, operating structure and intellectual property framework.

The intention is to establish an independently governed X-Lab capable of operating with substantial autonomy during Phase 1.

The X-Lab will retain direct control over:

- Technical research priorities.
- Recruitment and staffing.
- Allocation of research resources.
- Selection of external engineering partners.
- Intellectual property strategy.
- Experimental facilities and infrastructure.
- Commercialization and licensing decisions.

This autonomy is important because the project involves architectural research with uncertain outcomes. The ability to test alternative approaches, abandon unsuccessful ideas and redirect resources is essential.

### Partnerships and commercialization

The principal commercialization opportunity is the development and licensing of hardware intellectual property and associated design technology.

Potential customers and partners include:

- Semiconductor manufacturers.
- ASIC design organizations.
- Data-centre infrastructure providers.
- Database and enterprise software companies.
- Financial technology organizations.
- Scientific computing organizations.

The commercial model will initially emphasize licensing of reusable hardware intellectual property and associated design tools, with royalties or other arrangements appropriate to individual implementations.

The X-Lab will seek partnerships with semiconductor fabrication and design organizations to support prototype manufacture, testing and eventual deployment.

The intention is not to establish another conventional semiconductor manufacturing company. It is to establish a technology organization capable of developing new computational architectures and making those architectures available to existing semiconductor and computing industries.

### Conclusion

Database on a Chip addresses a fundamental architectural limitation of modern computing.

For decades, improvements in computing have largely been achieved by making processors faster, increasing memory capacity and improving communication between increasingly complex components.

The proposed X-Lab investigates a different possibility: eliminating much of the need for communication by making the data structure and the computation that operates on it a single physical entity.

The existing project provides a working starting point, an established development methodology and an initial hardware implementation.

NSF support would enable this foundation to be tested and extended by a multidisciplinary team, with independent benchmarking, prototype fabrication where feasible, and laboratory validation.

**The ultimate objective is to make data-centric computing a practical alternative to conventional processor-centric architectures, with potentially transformative consequences for the energy efficiency and economics of computing.**
